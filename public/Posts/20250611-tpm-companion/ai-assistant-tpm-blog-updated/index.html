How I Built an AI Assistant for TPMs: From Zoom Transcripts to Jira Tickets (and Beyond)

In December 2024, I started building the first version of the app. After testing it in January and February 2025, I demoed it to the Program Management community at CrowdStrike in March. I had hit a breaking point.

I was managing several high-stakes infrastructure and security programs, each with its own cadence of Zoom calls, sprawling Jira boards, Confluence pages that aged out fast, and stakeholder groups pinging me on Slack at all hours. I was burning too much time stitching information across systems—and too little time actually influencing outcomes.

So I built an AI assistant.

It wasn’t a moonshot. It was a pragmatic solution to a real problem: I needed help automating the coordination layer of program management—summarizing meetings, tracking development progress, identifying risks, and generating accurate status updates. And I needed to do it without sacrificing clarity, context, or trust. That assistant, powered by Claude 3.7 Sonnet, changed the way I work.

The Coordination Tax

Like most TPMs operating at scale, my calendar was a graveyard of context switches. In a typical week, I sat through dozens of meetings across engineering, product, security, and operations. I spent my evenings updating Jira, syncing Confluence pages, and formatting risk reports. It wasn’t sustainable.

Even with strong rituals and good hygiene, I found myself struggling to keep up with:

  • Unstructured conversations that needed summarizing
  • Decisions made verbally but never logged
  • Risk signals scattered across epics and sprints
  • Stakeholders asking for insights faster than I could type

The real problem wasn’t a lack of tools—it was the fragmentation of context. I needed a way to synthesize information across systems and surface what mattered, when it mattered.

Building the Assistant

I started with a simple hypothesis: Claude 3.7 Sonnet, with its long-context reasoning and low hallucination rate, could serve as the backbone of a TPM-focused coordination tool. From there, I built a lightweight Flask application to orchestrate inputs and outputs across the systems I touched daily.

The assistant consumed Zoom transcripts, monitored Jira activity, and pushed structured summaries and reports to Confluence and Slack.

From 6 to 15 Programs

Before building the assistant, I was managing 6 to 8 concurrent initiatives—a reasonable load, but one that left little headroom for growth. Within two months of deploying the tool, I was managing 15 programs in parallel.

The difference wasn’t just about scale—it was about focus. I was spending less time documenting and more time unblocking teams, resolving ambiguity, and driving execution.

The assistant didn’t replace the need for judgment—it gave me back the time to apply it.

What Worked

Claude’s ability to reason across long documents and interleave structured outputs made it the perfect match for TPM workflows. The model was particularly strong at detecting implicit decisions or surfacing risks that weren’t directly stated. When paired with disciplined prompt templates, it delivered consistent, trustworthy outputs that engineers and stakeholders quickly came to rely on.

Slack notifications and Confluence publishing made the assistant feel native to our existing workflows. People didn’t need to “use a new tool”—they just received better, faster updates where they already worked.

What Didn’t (Yet)

Of course, not everything was smooth. Transcripts from Whisper lacked reliable speaker attribution, which made some summaries ambiguous unless manually corrected. Claude occasionally hallucinated ticket status when Jira updates weren’t freshly pulled, so context freshness became a key constraint.

The dependency analysis feature was only as good as the teams’ ticket hygiene. If epics were loosely linked or descriptions vague, Claude had less signal to work with. And from a security perspective, I had to implement strict measures to ensure stateless processing, protect sensitive content, and meet internal audit standards.

A New Way to Lead

This project started as an experiment. It ended up transforming how I approached my role.

  • I reduced Jira update latency from days to minutes
  • I consistently delivered clear, accurate status reports ahead of schedule
  • I surfaced risks earlier—often before they were escalated
  • I doubled my program load without burnout
  • I re-centered my energy on solving problems, not tracking them

What Comes Next

There’s still more to build. I’m exploring:

  • Embedding-based memory so Claude can track program threads over time
  • Dynamic Jira querying with Claude’s upcoming tool-use API
  • Slack-side approval flows for one-click publishing
  • Hardened access controls and audit logging for enterprise adoption

But even in its current form, this assistant has redefined how I think about program management. I no longer see “AI” as a separate toolset—it’s a layer of leverage that sits alongside my instincts, processes, and leadership skills.

If you’re a TPM juggling multiple streams of work and trying to stay ahead of context collapse, this kind of assistant isn’t a luxury—it’s a necessity.

I didn’t set out to build an AI product.

I set out to build myself a better way to lead.
